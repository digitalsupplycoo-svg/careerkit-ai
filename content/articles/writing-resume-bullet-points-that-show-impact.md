Most resume bullet points describe a duty: "Responsible for managing social media accounts." A hiring manager
reading that has no idea whether you managed one account or twelve, whether engagement went up or down, or
whether the role mattered to the business at all. The fix isn't better adjectives — it's showing what changed
because you were there.

## The pattern: action, context, result

A strong bullet point usually has three parts: what you did, in what context, and what happened as a result. You
don't need all three in every bullet, but most weak bullets are missing the third part entirely.

Weak: "Responsible for managing social media accounts."

Better: "Grew Instagram following from 4,200 to 11,800 in eight months by shifting posting cadence from daily
promos to twice-weekly behind-the-scenes content."

The second version tells a reader what you actually changed and roughly how you did it, without needing a full
paragraph.

## What to do when you don't have a clean number

Not every job produces a tidy percentage. If you were a barista, a teacher's aide, or worked in a role without
obvious metrics, look for volume, scope, or a specific problem you solved instead.

- Volume: "Processed an average of 60 customer orders per shift with a 99% order-accuracy rate."
- Scope: "Trained 6 new hires on POS system and safety procedures during a staffing transition."
- Problem solved: "Redesigned the supply closet layout after repeated stockouts, cutting emergency reorders by
  roughly half."

Even a rough, honestly-estimated number ("roughly half," "an average of") is more useful to a reader than no
number at all — as long as it's a genuine estimate, not an invented one.

## Common mistakes

**Starting with a weak verb.** "Responsible for," "helped with," and "worked on" all bury the action. Lead with
what you actually did: "Led," "Rebuilt," "Negotiated," "Cut," "Designed."

**Listing tasks instead of outcomes.** "Attended weekly team meetings" is a task, not an outcome. If the meeting
mattered, say why: "Presented weekly inventory reports that flagged a recurring shortage two months before it
became a stockout."

**Padding with vague intensifiers.** Words like "successfully" or "effectively" don't tell a reader anything a
specific result wouldn't already show. Cut them and let the result speak for itself.

## A quick before-and-after example

Before: "Successfully helped improve the onboarding process for new employees."

After: "Rebuilt the new-hire onboarding checklist, cutting average ramp-up time from three weeks to nine
business days across two departments."

The "after" version is barely longer, but it answers the three questions a hiring manager actually has: what did
you do, how big was it, and what happened as a result.

## Checklist before you submit

- Does each bullet start with a strong action verb?
- Does at least half your bullets include a number, even a rough one?
- Could you defend every number in an interview if asked how you got it?
- Have you removed filler words like "successfully" and "responsible for"?
