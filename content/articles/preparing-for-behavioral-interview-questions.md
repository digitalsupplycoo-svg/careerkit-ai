Behavioral questions ask about specific past situations — "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager" —
because past behavior is a reasonably good predictor of future behavior. The most common failure isn't a lack of
good stories; it's telling them without enough structure for the interviewer to follow, or so rigidly scripted
that they sound rehearsed.

## The STAR structure, briefly

- **Situation:** one or two sentences of context — what was going on, and why it mattered.
- **Task:** what you specifically were responsible for or trying to achieve.
- **Action:** what you actually did, in enough detail to show your reasoning, not just the outcome.
- **Result:** what happened, ideally with a number or clear before/after.

The most commonly skipped part is Action — people rush from Situation straight to Result, which leaves the
interviewer unable to tell what *you* actually did versus what the team or circumstances did.

## Building a story bank before the interview

Instead of trying to guess every possible question, prepare 5–7 stories that each demonstrate a different theme:
handling conflict, solving a problem with limited resources, making a decision with incomplete information,
leading without formal authority, recovering from a mistake, managing competing priorities. Most behavioral
questions map onto one of these themes, so a single well-prepared story can answer several different phrasings
of a question.

## Example: turning a vague memory into a STAR answer

**Vague version:** "There was a time our team missed a deadline and I had to fix it."

**STAR version:**
- *Situation:* "Two weeks before a product launch, our design assets weren't finalized because the freelance
  designer we'd hired became unresponsive."
- *Task:* "As the project coordinator, I needed to either get the assets finished or find an alternative path to
  launch on time."
- *Action:* "I reached out to two backup freelancers same-day, sent them the existing brand guidelines and a
  tightly scoped brief, and split the remaining assets between them with a 4-day turnaround."
- *Result:* "We launched on the original date with no visible quality drop, and I built a two-freelancer backup
  list afterward so we wouldn't hit the same single point of failure again."

Notice the Result includes what changed going forward, not just the immediate fix — that second layer often
separates a good answer from a great one.

## Practicing without sounding memorized

Write bullet-point notes for each story, not a full script. Practice saying them out loud in different orders and
in response to different question phrasings ("Tell me about a conflict" vs. "Tell me about a time you had to
push back on a decision" might use the same story). This keeps the content consistent while the delivery stays
natural, because you're recalling facts rather than reciting lines.

## Handling a question you don't have a perfect story for

It's fine to take a moment: "Let me think of a good example for that." A brief pause is far less costly than
stretching an unrelated story to fit, which usually becomes obvious partway through.

## Common mistakes

- Speaking only in "we" and never clarifying your specific individual contribution
- Choosing a story where the result was mediocre or unresolved, without explaining what you learned
- Going over three minutes on a single answer — practice trimming to under two
- Badmouthing a former manager or company, even when the story genuinely involves conflict with them
