A layoff is common enough, across enough industries and company sizes, that most interviewers don't treat it as a red flag on its own the way they might have decades ago. What actually affects an interviewer's impression isn't the layoff itself — it's whether you talk about it with a clear, factual, unbothered tone, or whether it comes across as something you're still working through in real time during the conversation.

## State it plainly, without over-explaining

A short, factual statement is more effective than a long, detailed justification: "My role was eliminated as part of a company-wide restructuring in [month/year] — it affected [a specific number or team, if you can share it] and wasn't performance-related." One or two sentences is enough; over-explaining, especially unprompted, can accidentally signal more discomfort with the topic than the layoff itself would.

## If it was a broader layoff, say so specifically

If your layoff was part of a larger, well-known round — a company-wide reduction, a specific percentage of the workforce — naming that specifically ("part of the 15% reduction the company announced in [month]") is more reassuring to an interviewer than a vague reference to "some changes," because it's independently verifiable and clearly not about your individual performance.

## Don't criticize your former employer

Even if the layoff was handled poorly, or you have genuine grievances about how it happened, an interview isn't the venue to air them. A neutral, factual tone ("the company restructured after missing revenue targets") lands very differently than a frustrated or bitter one, even if both are technically accurate — interviewers read tone as a preview of how you'd talk about them if things ever went wrong at their company too.

## Address any resulting employment gap directly

If the layoff created a gap before your next role, briefly explaining what you did during that time — a focused job search, relevant learning, freelance work, caregiving, or simply a deliberate break — closes the loop without needing extensive justification. ## If you were let go for performance, not a layoff

This is a genuinely different, harder conversation, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which category actually applies before deciding how to frame it. If performance was a factor, a brief, honest acknowledgment paired with what you changed as a result ("there was a mismatch in expectations early on, and I've since made sure to clarify goals explicitly at the start of a new role") is more credible than implying it was purely a layoff when it wasn't — inconsistent stories are far more damaging if they unravel than an honest, brief acknowledgment ever would be.

## Prepare the answer before you need it

This question comes up often enough, directly or indirectly, that it's worth preparing a specific, rehearsed two-to-three-sentence answer rather than composing it live for the first time in an actual interview. Practice saying it out loud until the tone comes across as matter-of-fact rather than defensive — the words matter less than whether they sound rehearsed-calm versus rehearsed-nervous.

## The bigger picture

A layoff, framed clearly and without defensiveness, rarely costs a candidate an otherwise strong interview. What does cost candidates is visible discomfort, inconsistency between what's said and what's on the resume, or unprompted criticism of a former employer — all of which are avoidable with a short, honest, practiced answer.
