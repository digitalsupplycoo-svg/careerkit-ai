You've probably seen the claim that "70% of jobs are never posted." It shows up in career advice everywhere, almost always without a link to the actual study behind it — because in most cases, there isn't one. That doesn't mean the underlying idea is false. It means the popular version of it is a myth wearing a real phenomenon's clothes.

## What's actually true

Roles genuinely do get filled without ever going through a public job board — through internal transfers, referrals from current employees, or a hiring manager reaching out directly to someone they already know or were introduced to. This is well documented as a real pattern in how hiring works, especially for senior or specialized roles where the cost of a bad hire is high and referrals reduce that risk. What's not well documented is a precise, universal percentage for how common this is — it varies enormously by industry, company size, and seniority level, and any article citing one exact number as a fact about "the job market" broadly is almost certainly repeating an unsourced figure rather than citing real research.

## Why the exact number doesn't matter

Whether the real figure for your specific field and level is 30% or 60%, the practical advice is identical: if you only apply to postings you find on job boards, you're competing for a shrinking share of the roles that actually get filled, and you're invisible for the rest. The strategic response isn't to distrust every job board — plenty of real roles are filled that way too — it's to add a second channel that doesn't depend on someone posting a listing at all.

## How roles actually surface without a posting

- **Someone leaves or a team grows**, and the manager mentions it to a colleague or former coworker before HR ever opens a formal req.
- **A referral bypasses the general applicant pool** entirely, going straight to a hiring manager or skipping the initial screen.
- **A role gets created around a specific person** a company already wants, rather than the person being found to fill a pre-defined role.

None of this requires being famous or highly connected — it requires being visible enough, to enough of the right people, that you're the name that comes to mind when one of these situations happens.

## How to actually reach it

**Reconnect before you need something.** A message to a former colleague or manager that only appears when you're job hunting reads as transactional. Reaching out with a genuine, no-ask update ("saw you moved to a new role, congrats — hope it's going well") a few times a year keeps you visible without asking for anything, so when you do eventually mention you're looking, it doesn't feel like the first time they've heard from you in three years.

**Ask a specific, answerable question, not "know of anything?"** "Do you know of anything?" is easy to reflexively answer "not really" to, even when something does come to mind later. A more specific ask — "I'm looking at [specific type of role] at mid-size companies in [industry] — does that ring a bell for anyone on your team?" — gives the other person something concrete enough to actually think about.

**Talk to people doing the job you want, not just recruiters.** Recruiters are useful, but the people most likely to know about an unposted opening are the ones already doing similar work at companies you're interested in — they hear about team growth and departures before it's ever a listing.

**Still apply to posted roles.** Working the hidden channel doesn't mean abandoning job boards — it means not relying on them exclusively. ## The honest bottom line

The exaggerated statistic isn't harmless — it can make people distrust job boards entirely, or feel hopeless if they don't have an obvious network to lean on. The real, more useful version of the idea is simpler: some meaningful share of roles never reach a public posting, reaching them takes ongoing relationship-building rather than a single ask, and it's worth doing in addition to, not instead of, a normal job search.
