Most career changes fail not because the new field was a bad fit, but because the transition was planned backwards: quit first, figure it out later. A more reliable order is to test the new direction while still employed, then make the jump once there's real evidence it's worth making.

## Step 1: Separate "I dislike my job" from "I'd like that field"

These are different problems with different fixes. Sometimes the dissatisfaction is about a bad manager, a toxic team, or a mismatched role at a company you'd otherwise be fine at — a change of employer within the same field solves that. A genuine career change is worth pursuing when the actual work itself, not just the current context, isn't right for you. Ask what specifically you dislike day to day, and check whether the new field would remove that or just relocate it.

## Step 2: Talk to people already doing the job, not just about it

Job descriptions and career blogs describe a role's marketing pitch. A 20–30 minute conversation with someone actually doing the job tells you what the day-to-day is really like, including the parts that don't make it into a LinkedIn post. Ask what they spend most of their time on, what surprised them after switching in, and what they'd tell someone considering it. Most people are willing to have this conversation if you ask specifically and keep it short.

## Step 3: Test it before committing

Where possible, get a small, low-stakes sample of the new field before making a full transition:

- A short course or certification relevant to the field, evaluated honestly — did you enjoy the material, or just the idea of finishing it?
- A volunteer project, freelance gig, or side project that uses the actual skills of the new field.
- Shadowing or an informational interview embedded in a real workday, if someone will allow it.

This step exists to catch mismatches early, cheaply, before a resignation and months of income are on the line.

## Step 4: Identify what actually transfers

Very few career changes start from zero. Skills like project management, client communication, data analysis, writing, or people management often transfer directly, even if the job title changes completely. Make a specific list: not "communication skills" in the abstract, but the actual situations where you used them and what you produced. This list becomes the backbone of your resume and interview answers for the new field — it's the evidence that you're not starting over, even if your title is different.

Your [cover letter can connect those transferable skills](/articles/how-to-write-a-cover-letter-that-gets-read)
to the target role without pretending your previous title was something it was not.

## Step 5: Budget the transition realistically

Career changes usually come with a period of lower pay, additional training cost, or both. Before making the leap, estimate: how long can you sustain a pay cut or unpaid learning period, and what's the actual, not aspirational, timeline to get back to your current income in the new field? Being honest about this upfront prevents a rushed retreat back to the old field six months in out of financial pressure rather than genuine reconsideration.

## Step 6: Make the transition, don't just plan it forever

Testing and researching can become a way to avoid the risk of actually moving. At some point, with reasonable evidence gathered, the plan needs an execution date — applying to roles, enrolling in the program, or setting a notice date. If you find yourself endlessly researching without acting, it's worth asking honestly whether the hesitation is about the plan or about the change itself.

Once applications begin, use the [Job Application Tracker](/tools/job-tracker) to record which version of your
career-change story went to each employer and whether it led to a response. That gives you evidence to adjust the

A career change doesn't require certainty, which rarely exists in advance for any major decision. It requires enough evidence that the new direction addresses the actual problem, and a plan that doesn't bet everything on being right on the first try.
