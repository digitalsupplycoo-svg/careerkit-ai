Advice about interview body language often tips into vague pseudo-science — precise claims about exactly how many seconds of eye contact convey confidence, or what a specific hand gesture supposedly reveals. Most of that is overstated. What's genuinely true, and useful, is simpler: nervous body language is common, some of it is distracting enough to compete with your actual answers for attention, and a handful of specific, practical adjustments help more than trying to consciously control every movement.

## The goal isn't to perform confidence — it's to remove distractions

Trying to actively project confidence through posture and gesture usually reads as stiff or performative, especially on video. A more reliable goal: reduce the specific habits that pull attention away from what you're saying, so your actual answer is what the interviewer remembers, not a distracting tic.

## On video calls specifically

**Look at the camera, not the screen, when delivering a key point.** Looking at the person's face on screen feels natural but reads as slightly off-camera to them; looking directly at the camera lens during your most important sentences approximates eye contact much more closely, even though it feels unnatural to you while doing it.

**Frame yourself so your hands are visible if you gesture naturally.** A frame that's too tight, showing only your face, can make natural gestures look like off-screen fidgeting. This is a small technical fix — sit back slightly — that removes a distraction you might not even know you're creating.

**Check your lighting and background once, not every time.** A cluttered or poorly lit background pulls attention in the first thirty seconds, then usually fades — but a distraction that repeats (a device or notification visible behind you, a light causing glare) keeps pulling focus throughout.

## In person

**Sit back, not rigidly upright.** Leaning slightly forward when actively listening or making a key point, then returning to a relaxed, upright posture, reads as more natural than holding one stiff position for the entire interview.

**Keep your hands visible and let them move naturally when you talk.** Hiding your hands (crossed arms, hands under the table) can read as closed off, even unintentionally; forcing exaggerated gestures reads as performed. The natural middle ground — hands visible, moving normally as you'd gesture in any comfortable conversation — usually happens on its own once you stop actively thinking about it.

**Match the room's energy, not a fixed script.** A relaxed, conversational interviewer usually responds better to a genuinely relaxed match in tone; a more formal, fast-paced interviewer usually responds better to a slightly more measured, direct style. Rigidly performing "confident body language" regardless of the room's actual tone can read as tone-deaf.

## The single most common distracting habit

Filler words and verbal pacing matter as much as physical body language, and they're often the actual source of a "nervous" impression more than posture is. Speaking slightly slower than feels natural, and allowing a short pause before answering instead of filling every silence with "um" or an immediate half-formed answer, does more for a composed impression than any specific posture adjustment.

## Practice reduces this more than technique does

Most nervous body language fades on its own once the content of your answer is well-rehearsed enough that you're not simultaneously composing the answer and worrying about how you look. Practicing your answers out loud — ideally recording yourself once, even though it's uncomfortable to watch back — surfaces your own specific habits far more effectively than generic advice about posture ever will. ## The honest bottom line

Body language matters at the margins — it can add or subtract polish — but it rarely overrides the substance of your answers. Spending most of your preparation time on content, with a smaller amount on removing your own specific distracting habits, is a better use of limited prep time than memorizing generic gesture advice.
