Most cold outreach to a hiring manager gets ignored not because cold outreach doesn't work, but because most of it is generic, too long, or asks for too much too soon. A short, specific message that respects the other person's time and asks for one small, easy thing gets replies at a genuinely different rate than a long message asking to "pick your brain."

## Why most cold emails fail

The typical failed pattern: a long opening about your background before getting to the point, a vague ask ("would love to connect and learn more"), and no clear, low-effort action for the recipient to take. A busy hiring manager reads the first two lines, doesn't see an easy next step, and moves on — not out of rudeness, just triage.

## The structure that actually works

Keep it to four short parts: a specific, genuine reason you're reaching out to this person in particular, one sentence on relevant background, a specific and easy ask, and a low-friction way to say no without feeling rude. The whole message should be readable in under fifteen seconds.

## Template: reaching out about a specific open role

> Subject: Question about the [Job Title] role
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I saw the [Job Title] opening on your team and wanted to reach out directly — [one specific, genuine reason: "I noticed the role focuses on X, which is exactly the kind of work I've been doing at Y"].
>
> I'd love to apply, but wanted to ask directly: is there anything specific you're hoping to see in a strong application for this role?
>
> Totally understand if you're too busy to respond — I'll apply through the standard process either way. Thanks for your time.
>
> [Your name]

The "I'll apply either way" line matters — it removes any pressure to respond and makes replying feel like a bonus, not an obligation, which paradoxically makes people more likely to actually reply.

## Template: reaching out with no specific open role

> Subject: [Mutual connection / specific reason] — quick question
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> [Specific, genuine reason for reaching out — a mutual connection, a project of theirs you found genuinely interesting, a specific team you're curious about]. I'm exploring roles in [specific area] and your team came up as one I'd be excited about.
>
> No pressure at all, but if you ever have 15 minutes, I'd love to ask a couple of specific questions about what the team is working on. If not, no worries — I know how busy this time of year gets.
>
> [Your name]

## What makes the "specific reason" part actually work

Generic flattery ("I'm really impressed by your company") reads as templated because it could apply to any company. A specific, checkable detail — a particular project, a talk they gave, a specific product decision you have a genuine opinion about — signals you actually did the reading, not just found their email address. If you can't come up with one genuinely specific reason for reaching out to this particular person, that's worth noticing before sending anything.

## Following up once, not repeatedly

If you don't hear back within a week or so, one brief follow-up is normal and rarely annoys anyone: "Just following up in case this got buried — no worries if now isn't a good time." A second or third unsolicited follow-up after that starts to cost more goodwill than it's likely to gain.
