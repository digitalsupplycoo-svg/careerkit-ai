Declining a job offer is a small, easy-to-underestimate professional interaction — done well, it costs you nothing and can leave a genuinely positive impression; done poorly (going silent, dragging it out, or being needlessly harsh in your reasoning) it can close a door you might want open again someday, whether that's a future role, a reference, or a colleague who moves to a company you're interested in later.

## Decide promptly, then communicate promptly

Once you've made your decision, don't sit on it — a delayed decline, especially past a deadline you already agreed to, costs the company real time they could have spent moving to their next candidate. A same-day or next-day response once you're certain is both courteous and reflects well on you as someone who communicates clearly and doesn't waste other people's time.

## A simple, honest decline template

> Subject: [Job Title] Offer — Thank You
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Thank you again for the offer, and for the time everyone spent with me throughout the process — I genuinely enjoyed learning about the team and the role.
>
> After careful consideration, I've decided to accept a different opportunity that's a better fit for [one honest, brief reason — scope, location, timing]. This wasn't an easy decision, and I have a lot of respect for the team and what you're building.
>
> Thank you again for your time and consideration — I hope our paths cross again in the future.
>
> [Your name]

You don't owe a detailed explanation, and you don't need to name the other company or its exact offer unless you want to. A brief, honest, general reason is enough, and it's better than a vague non-reason that can read as evasive.

## Do it by phone or video first if you got to a final stage

If you had substantial conversations with a hiring manager, especially through multiple interview rounds, a brief call before the written decline is a meaningful courtesy — it takes more effort than an email, and that effort is itself part of what makes the gesture land well. A short call ("I wanted to tell you directly before sending anything in writing") followed by the same written note above covers both the personal and the formal side of it.

## What not to do

**Don't ghost.** Simply not responding to an offer, hoping it will be understood as a decline, is one of the more damaging things you can do professionally — it costs the company real time waiting on you, and it's remembered far more negatively than an honest, prompt decline ever would be.

**Don't use the decline as leverage for your other offer**, unless you're doing so transparently and are genuinely willing to accept if they counter. Implying you might reconsider for more money, without meaning it, wastes their time and can be seen through easily by an experienced hiring manager.

**Don't over-explain or criticize anything about the process or people**, even if you have real reservations — a decline is not the venue for detailed feedback unless it's specifically and genuinely requested, and even then, keep it constructive.
