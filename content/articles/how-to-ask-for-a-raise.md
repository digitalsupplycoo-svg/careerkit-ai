Asking for a raise without an outside offer in hand can feel like it has less leverage than a counteroffer negotiation, but most raises granted inside a company happen exactly this way — through a clear, evidence-based case, not an ultimatum. Threatening to quit unless you get a raise is a real risk: it can work once, but it changes the relationship afterward and only works if you're genuinely prepared to follow through.

## Build the case before the conversation

Vague statements ("I've been working really hard" or "I feel like I deserve more") are easy for a manager to acknowledge without acting on. A stronger case documents specifics: concrete accomplishments since your last raise or review, with results where possible, and how your responsibilities have grown compared to when your current pay was set. If you've taken on work outside your original job description, or your role has effectively expanded, name that explicitly rather than assuming your manager has tracked it as closely as you have.

Market data helps too, gathered from a couple of reputable salary sources for your role, level, and location. This isn't about presenting an ultimatum figure — it's about grounding your ask in something other than a feeling that you're underpaid.

## Timing matters more than people expect

The best time to ask is usually after a clear win — shipping a successful project, taking on new scope successfully, a strong performance review — not during a difficult period for the team or company, and not as an ambush in an unrelated meeting. If your company has a formal review cycle, raising it a few weeks before that cycle, so your manager has time to advocate for a number internally, tends to work better than raising it the week of.

## The conversation itself

State your ask directly rather than hinting at it. A structure that works: briefly summarize your recent contributions and expanded scope, state the specific raise or range you're asking for, and pause. Many people undercut their own ask by immediately following it with hedges ("but I understand if that's not possible right now") before the manager has even responded — let the ask sit.

If your manager needs to check with someone else or come back with an answer, that's normal for most raise requests above a small amount. Ask when you can expect to hear back, so the conversation doesn't quietly disappear.

## If the answer is no

A "no" is more useful when it comes with a reason. Ask directly: what would need to be true for a raise to be approved next cycle — specific scope, specific results, a specific timeline? This turns a rejection into a concrete plan rather than an open-ended wait, and it also tells you something important: if your manager can't or won't give you a clear answer to that question, it's a signal about how seriously future raise conversations will be taken.

## Why an ultimatum is different from a strong ask

A strong, evidence-based ask is not the same as "give me a raise or I'll quit." An ultimatum can work if you're truly prepared to leave and mean it, but it resets the relationship — some managers grant it and quietly start planning your eventual replacement, seeing the threat as a sign of instability rather than confidence. If you're not actually prepared to leave over the answer, don't frame the ask as if you are; make the strongest honest case instead, and treat a real competing offer, if you get one later, as a separate and different kind of conversation.
