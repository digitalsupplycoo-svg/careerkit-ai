The default LinkedIn connection request — "I'd like to add you to my professional network" — gets ignored constantly because it says nothing about why. Most people can tell within a sentence whether a message was written specifically for them or copy-pasted to fifty people that week, and the response rate difference between the two is large enough to be worth the extra thirty seconds of writing a real one.

## The core fix: one specific, checkable detail

A message becomes non-generic the moment it includes something the sender could only have written after actually looking at your profile — a specific role, a specific post you made, a shared connection, a specific company transition. Generic praise ("your background is impressive") could apply to almost anyone; a specific detail ("I saw you moved from [Company A] to [Company B] — I'm considering a similar move and would love your take") could only apply to that one person.

## Template: reaching out to someone in a role you want

> Hi [Name], I came across your profile while researching [specific role/field] — it looks like you made a similar move from [their previous background] a while back. I'm exploring a similar path and would love to ask a couple of quick questions if you ever have a few minutes. No worries at all if you're too busy — just wanted to reach out.

## Template: reconnecting with someone you haven't spoken to in a while

> Hi [Name], it's been a while — I saw you're now at [Company], congrats on the move. I've been [brief, genuine update]. No specific ask here, just wanted to say hello and see how things have been going.

Sending this kind of no-ask message periodically, not only when you need something, is what makes a later actual ask land as genuine rather than transactional — see our guide on <a href="/articles/hidden-job-market-myth-vs-reality">the hidden job market</a> for why this ongoing habit matters more than a single well-timed message.

## Template: following up after meeting someone in person

> Great meeting you at [event/context] — really enjoyed our conversation about [specific topic discussed]. Would love to stay in touch here.

Referencing the specific topic you discussed, rather than a generic "great meeting you," is what separates this from the dozens of similar connection requests the other person probably received that same week.

## What to avoid entirely

**Sending a pitch or ask in the very first message.** Asking for a referral, a job, or a favor before any actual conversation has happened reads as using the connection request as a shortcut, and it's the single fastest way to get ignored or declined.

**Copy-pasting the exact same message to many people in a short window.** Beyond the risk of someone comparing notes and noticing, a message written to work for anyone tends to read as written for no one in particular — the specificity that makes a message effective is inherently hard to mass-produce.

**Over-explaining your entire background before getting to the point.** Keep it to two or three sentences; a long opening message before any relationship exists is more likely to be skimmed or abandoned than read in full.

## Once the connection is made

A good opening message earns you a reply, not an instant favor — treat the resulting conversation as a real, unhurried exchange rather than rushing straight to an ask the moment they respond.
