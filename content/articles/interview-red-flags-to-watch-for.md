An interview is an evaluation in both directions, but candidates often focus entirely on being evaluated and forget to actually watch for signals about whether the job is a good one. Some of the clearest information about a role comes through in how the interview process itself is run, not just in what people say.

## Vague answers about the role

If multiple interviewers give inconsistent or noticeably vague answers to "what does success look like in this role after six months," that's often a sign the role itself is not well defined, or that the team doesn't agree internally on what they need. This isn't automatically disqualifying — some roles are genuinely still being shaped — but it's worth naming directly if you notice it, rather than assuming the vagueness will resolve itself once you're hired.

## High turnover in the role or team

Asking directly, "Why is this role open — is it new, or is it backfilling someone?" is a normal, expected question. If it's a backfill, a reasonable follow-up is what happened to the last person, and how long they were in the role. A pattern of short tenures in the same position across multiple hires is a stronger signal than any one person's story, since any individual might have left for reasons unrelated to the job.

## Disorganized or disrespectful process

How a company runs its interview process is a reasonable proxy for how it runs day to day. Repeated late cancellations, long unexplained silences between stages, or interviewers who are visibly unprepared and clearly haven't read your resume aren't automatically dealbreakers, but a consistent pattern across multiple touchpoints is more informative than a single scheduling hiccup, which can happen anywhere.

## Questions about workload and boundaries get deflected

Asking about typical working hours, how the team handles urgent issues outside of normal hours, or what a demanding week actually looks like is a legitimate, standard question, not an accusation. A direct, specific answer ("we have an on-call rotation, roughly one week in six, and it's genuinely rare to need an off-hours response") is a good sign. A defensive reaction, or an answer that avoids the actual question ("we work hard but we also have fun!") is worth noting, especially if it repeats across interviewers.

## Manager interview specifics

If you'll have a direct manager, the conversation with them is one of the most information-dense parts of the process. Ask how they give feedback, how they handled a recent disagreement with someone on their team, and what they think their management style is versus what a direct report might say about it. Answers that are abstract or entirely self-flattering, without a single concrete example, are less useful than a specific, honest answer that includes some acknowledgment of a real limitation.

## Comparing what you're told to what you can verify

Where possible, cross-check claims made during the interview against outside sources — employee reviews (read skeptically, since these skew toward extreme experiences in both directions), your own network for anyone who's worked there, or public information about the company's recent history. A single negative review means little; a consistent theme across many independent sources is worth taking seriously.

## None of this means walking away automatically

Red flags exist on a spectrum, and very few companies are entirely free of them. The goal of watching for these signals isn't to find a perfect employer — it's to go into a decision with clear eyes about the trade-offs you're accepting, rather than being surprised by something three months into the job that was actually visible during the interview process the whole time.
