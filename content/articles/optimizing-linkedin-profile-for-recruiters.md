Recruiters mostly find candidates through LinkedIn's internal search (Recruiter or the free search bar), not by browsing profiles at random. That changes what's worth optimizing: search matching and the first screen a recruiter sees after a match, not the whole page.

## What LinkedIn search actually matches on

Search weighs your headline, your current job title, your skills list, and the words in your experience section heavily. It does not deeply index your "About" paragraph the same way. This means two people with identical experience can have very different search visibility depending on whether their headline and skills list use the terms recruiters actually search for.

Practical fix: look at three to five real job postings for the role you want and note the exact terms they use for the role and key skills. If postings consistently say "Product Manager" and you've been calling yourself a "Product Owner," recruiters searching "Product Manager" won't find you. Use the terminology of the role you want next, not a more creative or internal title from your last company.

## The headline is not your job title by default

LinkedIn auto-fills your headline with your current title and company. Most people never change it, which wastes prominent, search-weighted real estate. A better headline names what you do and for whom, in plain language: "Backend Engineer — Payments & Fraud Systems" tells a recruiter more in five seconds than "Software Engineer II at [Company]."

If you're job hunting, it's fine for the headline to reflect the role you're targeting rather than your current title exactly, as long as it stays honest — for example, "Marketing Manager | Lifecycle & Retention" if that's genuinely your specialty, not an inflated title you don't have.

## Skills section: fewer, accurate, endorsed

LinkedIn allows many skills, but a long list dilutes the ones that matter and can look padded. Keep 15–20 skills that are genuinely central to the role you want, in priority order — the first few carry more weight in search and are what's visible without clicking "show more." Remove skills that were relevant three roles ago and aren't relevant to where you're going.

Endorsements matter less than people assume for search ranking, but a skill with zero endorsements next to one with forty can look neglected to a human reviewer. Asking two or three former colleagues to endorse your top skills is a five-minute favor most people are happy to do.

## The About section is for humans, not the algorithm

Once a recruiter clicks into your profile, the About section is often the first thing they read in full. Write it in first person, in plain sentences, not as a keyword-stuffed paragraph. Cover: what you do, one or two concrete examples of impact, and what you're looking for next, if you're open to it. Three to five short paragraphs is enough. Avoid generic openers like "Passionate professional with X years of experience" — it says nothing a recruiter hasn't read a thousand times.

## Experience entries: same principle as a resume

Each role should have two to four bullet points, ideally with a concrete result, not just a list of duties. This is the same discipline as writing resume bullet points — LinkedIn just gives you the chance to include a bit more context than a resume's tight space allows, such as the size of the team or the scope of the systems you worked on.

## Open to Work: public vs. recruiter-only

LinkedIn lets you signal "Open to Work" either publicly (a visible banner on your profile) or privately (visible only to recruiters using LinkedIn Recruiter). If you're currently employed and job searching discreetly, use the private setting — the public banner is visible to anyone including coworkers and your current manager's network.

## A profile photo and banner, briefly

Profiles with a photo get substantially more views in LinkedIn's own guidance, and a plain, well-lit headshot outperforms group photos, heavy filters, or no photo at all. The banner image is often left as the default; a simple banner related to your field (not required, but low effort) makes the profile feel more complete without any design skill needed.

None of this replaces having real experience and clear communication — it just makes sure the profile doesn't undersell what's already true about your background.
