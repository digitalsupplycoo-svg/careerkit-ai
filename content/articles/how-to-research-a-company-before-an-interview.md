Reading a company's "About" page and homepage is the most common form of interview preparation, and also one of the least useful — that page is marketing copy, written to sound good, not to give you material for an actual conversation. Useful company research produces something specific enough to reference in an answer or ask about directly; generic research produces facts that never come up because there's no natural moment to mention them.

## Start with what the company actually says about itself, beyond the homepage

Recent public statements — a founder or executive interview, a blog post about a specific product decision, an earnings call summary if the company is public — tend to reveal more of what the company actually prioritizes right now than a static About page, which is often years old and rarely updated. Look specifically for language the company uses to describe its own current priorities or challenges; that language, reflected back in your own answers, signals that you did real, current research rather than a five-minute skim.

## Check what current and former employees say, read skeptically

Employee review sites are useful for pattern-spotting, not individual data points — one glowing or scathing review means little, but a consistent theme repeated across many independent reviews (a specific complaint about pace, or a specific praise for how a team operates) is worth taking seriously and, if relevant, asking about directly in a diplomatic way during the interview itself.

## Understand what the team you're joining actually does, specifically

The team or department you'd join matters more for your day-to-day experience than the company's overall reputation. If you can find any specific, recent information about that team — a project they've shipped, a specific challenge mentioned in a job posting or public content — that's more useful preparation than broad company facts, because it lets you ask a specific, informed question rather than a generic one.

## Research the interviewers themselves, briefly and professionally

A quick look at a public professional profile for each interviewer — their role, how long they've been there, any shared background or connection — takes a few minutes and can surface a genuine, specific conversation starter. This isn't about deep research into someone's personal life; it's the same professional-context research you'd naturally do before any important meeting.

## Look for what the company isn't saying

A company's public content, job postings, and reviews sometimes reveal gaps worth noting — a role posted repeatedly (possible high turnover), a job description that reads as unusually vague about actual responsibilities, or public reviews consistently flagging the same specific concern. See our guide on <a href="/articles/job-description-red-flags-you-should-never-ignore">job description red flags you should never ignore</a> for how to read a posting itself for these signals before you even get to the interview stage.

## Turn research into actual material, not trivia

The point of research isn't to recite facts back at the interviewer — it's to generate two things: a genuinely specific reason you're interested in this role at this company (not a generic one that could apply anywhere), and two or three specific, informed questions that show you did real homework. A question like "I noticed you recently shifted toward [specific initiative] — how has that changed what the team prioritizes day to day?" does more work than any amount of memorized company history.

## A simple research checklist before any interview

- One recent, specific public statement from the company (not the About page)
- Any pattern across multiple employee reviews, read skeptically
- What the specific team you'd join actually works on
- A brief look at each interviewer's public professional background
- Two to three specific, informed questions prepared in advance
