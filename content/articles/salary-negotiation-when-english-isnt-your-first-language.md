Negotiating in a second language adds a real layer of difficulty on top of an already uncomfortable conversation — not because your English isn't good enough, but because negotiation relies heavily on tone, pacing, and slightly indirect phrasing that doesn't always translate the way it's meant to. The fix isn't more fluency; it's preparing the specific language in advance so you're not composing a delicate sentence in real time.

## The core problem isn't vocabulary

Most non-native speakers negotiating in English already know the words they need. The actual difficulty is usually one of two things: translating a native-language politeness pattern directly into English and having it land as either too blunt or too hesitant, or losing confidence mid-sentence and softening a clear ask into a vague one without meaning to. Both are solvable by preparing exact phrases in advance, rather than trying to improvise diplomatic language under pressure.

## Prepare your exact opening line, word for word

Don't just prepare the idea — prepare the literal sentence, and practice saying it out loud until it feels automatic rather than translated in your head in the moment. A reliable, neutral structure:

> "Thank you for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about this role. Based on my research and experience, I was hoping we could discuss a base salary closer to [specific number]."

This sentence works because it's short, direct, and doesn't rely on subtle tone to carry its meaning — it says exactly what it means, which removes the risk of a phrase landing more hesitant or more aggressive than you intended.

## Avoid over-apologizing, a common cross-language pattern

Many languages use polite hedging ("I'm so sorry to ask, but...", "I don't want to be difficult, but...") far more heavily than professional English negotiation typically does. Directly translated, this can make a reasonable, standard ask sound like an apology for having needs at all. A useful rule: state the ask first, in one direct sentence, before adding any softening language — "I'd like to ask about the base salary — I think there's room to discuss [number] given [reason]" rather than leading with three sentences of apology before the actual ask appears.

## If you lose your train of thought mid-conversation

It's completely normal, in any language, to blank momentarily during a live negotiation. Two prepared phrases make this recoverable without losing ground:

> "Let me make sure I say this clearly — could I take a moment to think that through?"
>
> "I want to be precise about this, would it be alright if I follow up in writing with the specific number I have in mind?"

Both are professional in any context, not just for non-native speakers, and the second is genuinely useful strategically: writing gives you time to phrase your ask exactly right without the pressure of live conversation, and a written follow-up is completely normal after a verbal offer discussion.

## Numbers are language-independent — lean on them

A specific number, clearly stated, communicates regardless of accent or phrasing precision: "$85,000" means the same thing said any way. Prepare your target number and your reasoning as short, simple sentences rather than elaborate ones — "Based on [source], the market range for this role is $X to $Y; I'd like to be at $Z" is clear and doesn't require complex grammar to land correctly. ## It's fair to ask for accommodation

If a live negotiation over video or phone is genuinely harder for you than in writing, it's reasonable to ask: "Would it be possible to continue this conversation over email so I can be precise about the details?" Most employers will accommodate this without a second thought — it reads as thorough, not as a weakness.

## The broader point

The mechanics of salary negotiation — research a range, state a specific number, let the ask sit without immediately undercutting it — are the same regardless of what language you're negotiating in.
