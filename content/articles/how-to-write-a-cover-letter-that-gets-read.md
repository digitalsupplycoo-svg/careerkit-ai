A recruiter often spends less than a minute on a cover letter, if they open it at all. That doesn't mean cover
letters are pointless — it means the first two sentences have to earn the rest of the read. Most cover letters
fail at exactly that point by opening with something the recruiter already knows.

## The opening most people write (and should avoid)

"I am writing to apply for the Marketing Coordinator position at Acme Corp, which I saw posted on LinkedIn."

The recruiter already knows what role this is and where it was posted — that's why the letter is in this
folder. This sentence spends your one shot at attention on information with zero value to the reader.

## A better opening: lead with relevance

Open with the single most relevant thing about you for this specific role — a result, a shared context, or a
concrete connection between your background and their need.

**Example:** "In my last role, I rebuilt a client onboarding process that cut average setup time from three weeks
to nine days — the same bottleneck your job posting describes for the Client Success Coordinator role."

This tells the reader, in one sentence, why they should keep reading.

## Structure that works

1. **Opening (1–2 sentences):** the most relevant result or connection, tied directly to something in the
   posting.
2. **Body (2–3 short paragraphs):** one or two specific examples that demonstrate you can do what the role
   requires. Don't restate your resume — expand on one or two things it can't fully explain, like *why* you made
   a decision or *how* you handled a specific problem.
3. **Close (2–3 sentences):** a specific, low-pressure next step and genuine interest in the company, not a
   generic "I look forward to hearing from you."

## Example body paragraph

"When our team lost our main supplier two weeks before a product launch, I built a shortlist of three backup
vendors within 48 hours, negotiated a rush order with the one that could meet our timeline, and we shipped on
schedule. I'd bring that same instinct for finding a workable path under pressure to your operations team."

Notice this paragraph doesn't just say "I'm good under pressure" — it shows a specific situation, the action
taken, and the outcome, the same pattern that makes resume bullets work.

## What to leave out

- Restating your entire resume in paragraph form
- Generic praise ("Your company is a leader in innovation...") that could apply to any company
- Salary expectations, unless the posting explicitly asks for them
- Apologizing for a gap or lack of experience — address it briefly and factually if relevant, without dwelling

## When a cover letter isn't required

If a posting says a cover letter is optional, a short, well-targeted one can still help you stand out — but a
generic, clearly copy-pasted letter can hurt more than submitting none at all. If you don't have time to tailor
it to this specific role, it's often better to skip it than to submit something obviously generic.

## Quick checklist

- Does the opening line reference something specific to this company or role?
- Does the body include at least one concrete example, not just claims?
- Is it under one page — ideally 250–350 words?
- Have you replaced every instance of a previous company's name if this is a reused draft?
