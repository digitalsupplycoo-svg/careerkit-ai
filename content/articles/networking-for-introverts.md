Networking advice is often written for people who find rooms full of strangers energizing. If that's not you, the standard advice — work the room, hand out business cards, make small talk with everyone — can feel not just uncomfortable but genuinely ineffective, because it's optimized for a style of interaction you're fighting against the whole time.

## Depth over breadth

Introverts often network more effectively through fewer, deeper conversations than through wide but shallow ones. Instead of trying to meet as many people as possible at an event, pick two or three people to have a real conversation with — about their work, a shared interest, or a specific question you're genuinely curious about. A 20-minute conversation that one person actually remembers afterward is worth more than fifteen 90-second exchanges no one remembers.

## One-on-one beats group settings

If large events drain you, one-on-one coffee chats or calls are a completely legitimate substitute, not a lesser version of networking. Reaching out individually — to a former colleague, someone in your field whose work you find interesting, an alum from your school — and asking for 20 minutes of their time works well precisely because it removes the group dynamic that's the actual source of the discomfort, not the networking itself.

## Written outreach plays to a different strength

A well-written message can do a lot of the work an in-person introduction would, and writing is a channel many introverts are more comfortable and more articulate in than live conversation. A short, specific message — referencing something concrete about the person's work, stating clearly why you're reaching out, and asking for something small and low-effort (a 15-minute call, a quick question answered) — gets a better response rate than a vague "let's connect."

## Prepare a few real questions in advance

Some of the discomfort in networking conversations comes from the pressure to generate conversation in real time. Having two or three genuine questions ready in advance — about someone's career path, a decision they made, or their view on a trend in the field — removes a lot of that pressure and often leads to a more interesting conversation than improvised small talk would.

## Existing relationships are networking too

Networking doesn't only mean meeting new people. Staying in occasional touch with former colleagues, classmates, or managers — a message when they change jobs, a relevant article shared without an ask attached — maintains a network that's often more useful than a room of strangers, because there's already trust and context. This kind of networking is quieter, slower, and plays to consistency rather than social energy.

## Give before you ask

A message that offers something — a useful article, an introduction to someone else, specific praise for a piece of work — before ever asking for anything tends to land better than a cold ask, and it doesn't require performing enthusiasm you don't feel. Genuine, specific interest reads clearly even in a short message.

## The goal isn't to become extroverted

The point of low-pressure networking isn't to fake a personality you don't have — interviewers and professional contacts can generally tell when someone is performing energy rather than being genuinely engaged. The goal is finding a version of relationship-building that's sustainable enough that you'll actually keep doing it, since consistency over time matters more than any single event or conversation.
