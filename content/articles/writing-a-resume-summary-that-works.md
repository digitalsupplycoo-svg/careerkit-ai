A resume summary sits at the top of the page, which means it either earns a recruiter's attention for the rest of the document or wastes the most valuable few seconds of their read. Most summaries fail because they describe a category of person ("results-driven professional") instead of communicating anything specific about the one person whose resume it actually is.

## What a summary is for

A resume summary should answer one question quickly: who is this person, professionally, and what would make them a strong fit for this kind of role? It's not a mission statement, and it's not a place to restate your job title. Done well, it functions like a thesis statement for the rest of the resume — everything below should support the claim made in these two or three sentences.

## The generic version, and why it fails

"Dedicated and hardworking professional with 5 years of experience seeking to leverage my skills in a challenging new role" could be attached to almost any resume, for almost any role, by almost any candidate. It contains no information a recruiter can use to differentiate this resume from the next hundred in the stack. If a sentence in your summary could be copy-pasted onto someone else's resume without sounding wrong, it's not doing any work.

## A structure that holds up

A useful summary usually has three parts, in a sentence or two each:

1. **Who you are, specifically** — role and area of focus, not just a job title. "Backend engineer focused on payments infrastructure" says more than "Software Engineer."
2. **A concrete signal of scope or result** — the size of what you've worked on, a notable outcome, or years of experience in the specific area that matters for this role, not just total years worked.
3. **What you're aiming at**, if relevant — especially useful for a career changer or someone targeting a slightly different role than their current one, since it tells the reader how to interpret the rest of the resume.

Example: "Backend engineer with six years building and scaling payments infrastructure, including a rebuilt fraud-detection pipeline that reduced processing latency significantly. Looking to bring that experience to a senior infrastructure role at a growth-stage fintech company."

## When to skip the summary

Not every resume needs one. Early-career resumes with limited work history often don't have enough specific material yet to make a summary worthwhile — in that case, the space is often better used for an expanded education or projects section. A summary is most valuable when you have enough real experience to make a specific, differentiated claim, or when your target role isn't obvious from your most recent job title alone.

## Tailoring it per application

A generic summary written once and left unchanged across every application defeats its own purpose, since the strongest version speaks directly to the specific role. This doesn't require rewriting from scratch each time — most people keep two or three versions calibrated to the types of roles they're applying for, and adjust the emphasis (which accomplishment to lead with, which skill to name) to match the specific posting.

## What to leave out

Avoid generic adjectives with no supporting evidence ("passionate," "dynamic," "results-driven") unless they're immediately followed by something concrete that demonstrates the claim. A recruiter reading "results-driven" learns nothing; a recruiter reading the actual result learns what you're capable of.
