A panel interview — multiple interviewers in the room or on the call at once — asks for a few adjustments beyond standard interview preparation, mostly around managing attention and information across more than one person at a time.

## Get the roster in advance if you can

If a recruiter tells you who's on the panel, ask for names and roles ahead of time. Knowing that one panelist is a future peer, another a cross-functional stakeholder, and another the hiring manager lets you anticipate what each is likely to probe — a peer often asks about day-to-day collaboration, a stakeholder about how you'd handle their team's specific needs, a hiring manager about overall fit and judgment. If you can't get names in advance, ask each panelist their role at the start, which is a normal, expected question.

## Direct your answer, but don't ignore the room

When one panelist asks a question, it's natural to answer primarily to them, but briefly including the rest of the panel with eye contact (in person) or attention (on video) keeps everyone engaged rather than watching a private conversation. This doesn't require a formal technique — a glance toward the group every sentence or two is enough.

## Panels often split up topics

Interviewers on a panel sometimes coordinate in advance to each own a different area (technical skills, culture fit, past experience), which means you may get asked overlapping versions of a similar question from different angles. This is normal, not a sign you already answered it wrong the first time — each person may be probing a slightly different aspect even if the surface question sounds similar.

## Notice group dynamics, briefly

How panelists interact with each other — whether they seem aligned, whether one clearly defers to another, whether there's visible tension — is a small window into team dynamics that a one-on-one interview doesn't offer. It's not the main thing to focus on, but if you notice something notable, it's fair to factor it into your own evaluation of the role afterward.

## Managing your energy across a longer format

Panel interviews are sometimes longer than individual rounds, or come packed into a single day alongside several one-on-one interviews. Pacing yourself matters — keeping early answers reasonably concise so you have energy and focus left for later questions, and asking for a short break between sessions if you're given a multi-hour loop and genuinely need one. Most interviewers won't think less of a reasonable request for a two-minute break during a long day.

## Following up after a panel

A single thank-you note to the recruiter, or brief individual notes to each panelist if you have their contact information, both work. If sending individual notes, make each one specific to something discussed with that person rather than sending an identical message to everyone, which is easy for panelists to compare notes on afterward if they happen to mention it to each other.

## The core skills are the same

Despite the format differences, a panel interview is still evaluating the same things a one-on-one interview would: clear communication, relevant experience, and good judgment under a bit of pressure. The format adjustments here are about presentation and logistics, not a fundamentally different kind of preparation — the content of strong answers doesn't change based on how many people are in the room.
