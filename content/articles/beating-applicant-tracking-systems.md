An Applicant Tracking System (ATS) is software that companies use to collect, store, and search resumes. Despite
the popular image of a strict gatekeeper that auto-rejects resumes for missing keywords, most modern ATS
platforms are closer to a searchable database with some parsing and filtering built in. Understanding what the
software actually does — and doesn't do — helps you format sensibly instead of guessing.

## What an ATS does

When you submit a resume, the ATS extracts text from your file and tries to sort it into fields: name, contact
info, work history, education, skills. A recruiter can then search that database by keyword, filter by years of
experience, or sort by application date.

Some systems also generate a rough "match score" against the job description, which a recruiter may or may not
actually use. Very few companies auto-reject every resume below a score threshold without any human ever seeing
it — but poor formatting can still cause your resume to be parsed incorrectly, which has the same practical
effect as rejection.

## What breaks parsing

- **Tables and text boxes.** Some parsers read tables in the wrong order or skip text boxes entirely, scrambling
  your work history.
- **Headers and footers.** Contact info placed in a document header or footer is sometimes dropped by parsers.
- **Unusual section headings.** "My Journey" instead of "Experience" may not be recognized as a section at all.
- **Images of text.** If your resume is a scanned image or a screenshot pasted into a document, there's no
  extractable text at all.
- **Multi-column layouts.** Columns can be read left-to-right across the whole line instead of top-to-bottom
  within each column, jumbling job titles and dates.

## How to format for both software and humans

Use a single-column layout with standard section headings ("Experience," "Education," "Skills"), plain bullet
points instead of tables, and a PDF exported directly from a word processor rather than a scanned image. This
format reads cleanly in almost every parser and is also just easier for a human to skim.

## About keywords

Matching language from the job posting helps, but not because of a magic keyword quota. If a posting says
"Google Analytics" and your resume says "web analytics tools," a keyword search for the former won't find you —
not because you lack the skill, but because the exact phrase isn't present. The fix is simple: when a posting
names a specific tool, methodology, or certification you actually have, use that exact term somewhere in your
resume.

**Example:** A posting requires "experience with SQL and Tableau." If your resume currently says "built dashboards
using various BI tools," rewrite it to name the specific tools: "Built weekly sales dashboards in Tableau, sourced
from SQL queries against a 40-table warehouse."

Do not simply list every keyword from a posting in a hidden or oversized font — this is against most platforms'
terms, doesn't fool a human reviewer, and can look like keyword stuffing if a recruiter opens the raw file.

## Quick pre-submission check

- Single column, no tables or text boxes around your work history
- Contact info in the main body, not a header/footer
- Standard section headings
- Exported as a real PDF from a text document, not a scanned image
- Job title and key required tools named explicitly if you actually have that experience
