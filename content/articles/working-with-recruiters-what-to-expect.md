Recruiters are often treated either as a shortcut to a job or as gatekeepers to route around, and both framings miss what the relationship actually is: a recruiter is working toward filling a specific set of roles, and being useful to that goal is what makes the relationship work well for you too.

## In-house vs. agency recruiters

An in-house (or "corporate") recruiter works directly for a company and is typically trying to fill roles at that one organization. An agency (or "external") recruiter works for a staffing or recruiting firm and is usually paid a fee by the hiring company when they place a candidate successfully, which means their incentive is tied to a placement happening, not necessarily to finding you the single best-fit role among everything available. Neither type is inherently better to work with, but understanding the incentive helps calibrate how much weight to put on their enthusiasm about a specific role.

## What a recruiter can realistically do

A good recruiter can get your resume seen by an actual hiring manager instead of sitting in a large applicant pool, give you real information about the role and team that isn't in the job posting, coach you on what that specific interview process tends to look like, and sometimes advocate for you internally on compensation. This is genuinely valuable and worth taking seriously.

## What a recruiter can't do

A recruiter can't make a hiring manager choose you over a stronger candidate, and a good one won't promise that they can. Be cautious of anyone who guarantees an outcome, pressures you to accept an offer quickly without time to consider it, or pushes you toward a role that doesn't match what you've said you're looking for — that's usually a sign their placement incentive is outweighing your interests in that specific interaction.

## Being a good client, not just a candidate

Recruiters manage a pipeline of candidates and roles, and being clear and responsive makes their job easier, which tends to translate into them prioritizing you when a good match comes up. Being specific about what you want (role type, salary range, location or remote requirements, deal-breakers) upfront saves both of you time compared to vague answers followed by declining every role they bring you. If a recruiter presents an option that's clearly not a fit, a quick, direct "not a match because X" is more useful to the relationship than silence or a slow no.

## Salary conversations with a recruiter

Recruiters often ask for your current salary or salary expectations early. Where legal (this varies by location — some places restrict employers from asking current salary), you're not obligated to share your exact current number and can redirect to your expected range for the new role instead. Giving a well-researched range, rather than a single number, keeps some room for negotiation later without appearing unprepared.

## Working with more than one recruiter

There's nothing wrong with working with multiple recruiters or agencies simultaneously during a job search, but keep track of which roles you've been submitted for through which recruiter — being submitted to the same role by two different agencies creates an awkward situation for the hiring company and can cost you the opportunity entirely. If a recruiter mentions a specific company, check whether another recruiter has already submitted you there before agreeing to a second submission.

## After the search ends

If you place well with a recruiter's help, staying in occasional touch is worth it — the relationship is often useful again for your next search, and agency recruiters in particular tend to specialize in a niche, so a recruiter who placed you well once may be one of the more efficient paths back into the market the next time you need it.
