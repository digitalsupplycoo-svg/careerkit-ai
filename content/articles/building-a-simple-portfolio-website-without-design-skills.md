A common reason people avoid building a portfolio site is assuming it needs to look professionally designed, which turns a straightforward task into an intimidating one. A clean, simple, text-forward page showcasing real work clearly beats an ambitious design attempt that never gets finished — the actual work is what's being evaluated, not your web design skill, unless web design is the job you're applying for.

## Decide what the site actually needs to do

Before building anything, get specific about the goal: show a small number of strong work samples with enough context that a reviewer understands what you did and why it mattered, and make it easy to contact you or find your resume. Everything beyond that — animations, a custom design, an elaborate navigation structure — is optional polish that can wait, and often isn't worth the time investment for a portfolio's actual purpose.

## Use a simple, free tool rather than building from scratch

Unless you're specifically applying for a role where custom-built web development is itself the skill being demonstrated, there's no need to write code from scratch. A simple site builder, a single well-organized page on a free hosting platform, or even a clearly organized public document with links to your work is a completely legitimate portfolio for most fields — reviewers care about the content, not the platform it's hosted on.

## Structure that works for almost any field

- **A short intro**, two or three sentences: who you are, what you do, and what kind of work you're looking for.
- **Three to six work samples**, each with one or two sentences of context: what the project was, your specific role, and the outcome if you can share it.
- **A clear way to contact you** — an email address or a link to a professional profile, placed somewhere obvious, not buried at the bottom of a long page.

Resist the urge to include everything you've ever made. A focused page with your best few pieces, each with real context, outperforms a long page with everything and context for nothing.

## Writing context that actually helps a reviewer

For each piece, answer three questions in a sentence or two: what was the goal, what did you specifically do (especially if it was a team project — name your actual contribution), and what happened as a result, if you know. "Redesigned the onboarding email sequence, which reduced early drop-off" tells a reviewer far more than a screenshot with no explanation at all.

## Keep it current

An outdated portfolio with your oldest, weakest work still featured prominently undersells your current skill level more than having no portfolio at all. Set a reminder to revisit it every few months, swapping in more recent work and removing anything that no longer represents your best current level — a portfolio is a living document, not a one-time project to finish and forget.

## What to skip, at least at first

Custom domain names, elaborate animations, and a fully unique design are all nice-to-haves that can come later if you decide the portfolio is worth investing more in. None of them are worth delaying publishing a simple, clear version — a live, simple portfolio beats a perfect one that's still unfinished six months later.
