A strong reference list is often treated as an afterthought — three names and phone numbers thrown together at the last minute — even though a reference call happens at the final stage of a hiring decision, when a company is close to an offer and looking for a reason to confirm the choice, not looking for a reason to reject it. A well-chosen, well-prepared reference reinforces that confidence; an unprepared one, caught off guard, can introduce doubt at the worst possible moment.

## Who actually makes a strong reference

A direct former manager is usually the strongest reference, since they can speak to your actual day-to-day performance and growth with real authority. A senior peer or a cross-functional partner you worked with closely can be a strong second or third reference, especially if they can speak to collaboration or a specific project a manager might not have been as close to. A reference who barely worked with you, even if their title is impressive, is weaker than someone with a real, specific working relationship regardless of seniority.

## Always ask before listing someone

Never list a reference without asking first, ideally with real advance notice rather than a rushed message right before you need them contacted. Beyond simple courtesy, asking in advance also tells you something important: hesitation in someone's response ("uh, sure, I guess so") is a genuine signal that they might not give you the strongest reference, and it's better to learn that before a company calls them, not after.

## Give every reference real context before they're contacted

A reference who's caught off guard, without knowing what role you're applying for or what to emphasize, gives a vaguer, less useful reference than one who's had five minutes to think about it in advance. Before listing someone, send a short message:

> "Hi [Name], I wanted to give you a heads-up that I've listed you as a reference for a [Job Title] role at [Company]. If you're contacted, it would be great if you could speak to [one or two specific things — a project you worked on together, a specific skill]. Happy to send more detail if useful, and thank you again for being willing to do this."

This does two things: it confirms they're still comfortable being listed, and it primes them to mention the specific things most relevant to the role you're applying for, rather than whatever comes to mind first.

## Match the right reference to the right role

If you're applying for a role that emphasizes a specific skill or project, choose the reference who can speak to that specific thing most directly, rather than defaulting to the same three people for every application. A reference who directly witnessed the exact kind of work the new role requires is more useful than a more senior reference with a vaguer, more general impression of you.

## What to do about a reference you're unsure about

If your most recent manager isn't someone you're confident will give a strong reference — a strained relationship, a role that ended badly — it's reasonable to lean more heavily on other references (a peer, an earlier manager, a client) rather than including someone you have real doubts about, especially if you have other strong options. If you're asked directly why a specific recent manager isn't listed, a brief, neutral answer ("we didn't work together long enough for them to speak to my work in depth" or something similarly true and non-negative) is enough; you don't need to explain a difficult relationship in detail.
