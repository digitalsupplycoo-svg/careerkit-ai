"Tell me about yourself" is usually the first question in an interview, and it sets the tone for everything that
follows. It isn't an invitation to recite your entire life story or your resume line by line — the interviewer
already has your resume. It's a chance to frame your background around the role in front of you.

## A structure that works: present, past, future

**Present:** Start with where you are right now, briefly — your current role and the core of what you do.

**Past:** Give a short, relevant path to how you got here — one or two career moves that build toward this role,
not a full chronology.

**Future:** End by connecting to why you're interested in this specific role or company.

## Example

"I'm currently a customer support lead at a 40-person software company, where I manage a team of five and handle
escalations for our biggest accounts. Before that, I spent two years as a support rep myself, which is where I
got hands-on with the day-to-day problems our customers actually run into. I started looking for my next step
because I want more ownership over how a support team is built and measured, not just how it's run day to day —
which is exactly what this Head of Support role seems to offer."

This answer takes about 45 seconds to say out loud and gives the interviewer three useful pieces of information:
where you are, why you're credible, and why you want this specific role — without narrating a full resume.

## How long should it be?

Aim for 60–90 seconds. Longer than that and you risk losing the interviewer's attention before the interview has
even really started; shorter and you may seem underprepared or disengaged.

## Tailoring it per interview

Keep a consistent core (your present and past sections rarely need to change) but adjust the "future" section for
each company. If you're interviewing at a smaller startup versus a large enterprise, the reason you want that
specific role should sound different, because the actual reasons probably are different.

## What to leave out

- Personal life details unrelated to your professional background, unless the interviewer specifically asks
- A full chronological work history — pick the two or three moves that actually build a narrative
- Negative commentary about a previous employer, even in passing
- Reciting your resume verbatim; the interviewer is looking at it already

## Practicing it

Say it out loud, not just in your head — written and spoken language sound different, and a script that reads
well can sound stiff aloud. Record yourself once if you can stand to; it's an efficient way to catch filler words
and pacing issues you won't notice otherwise.

## A quick variant: "Walk me through your resume"

This is a close cousin, usually asking for a bit more chronological detail. The same present-past-future
structure works, but you can allow slightly more time on the "past" section, spending a sentence on each major
role and what it led to next.
