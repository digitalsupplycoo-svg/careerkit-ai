Most advice about becoming "senior" describes it as doing your current job better — faster, with fewer mistakes, more independently. That's part of it, but it undersells the real gap, which is less about skill level and more about what you're actually being evaluated on. A junior role is judged mostly on execution; a senior role is judged on judgment, and those require genuinely different habits, not just more practice at the same ones.

## Execution vs. judgment

At a junior level, the core question is usually "can you do the task correctly, given clear direction?" At a senior level, the question shifts to "can you figure out what the right task even is, with incomplete direction, and know when to escalate versus decide yourself?" This is why someone can be excellent at the technical mechanics of a job and still get passed over for a senior title — the mechanics were never the bottleneck.

## Ambiguity tolerance is a trainable skill, not a personality trait

Junior roles are often deliberately structured with clear inputs and expected outputs. Senior roles regularly involve situations with no clear right answer, conflicting priorities, or incomplete information — and the specific skill being evaluated is whether you can make a reasonable call anyway, explain your reasoning, and adjust if new information contradicts it. This is genuinely learnable: deliberately taking on ambiguous, loosely scoped tasks and practicing making a documented, reasoned call — rather than waiting for someone to fully define the problem first — builds this muscle directly.

## Influence without formal authority

A senior-level expectation that catches many people off guard: getting other people, sometimes outside your direct team, to actually act on your recommendation — without having any authority to simply tell them to. This requires a different skill set than doing excellent individual work: building a case with evidence, understanding what a specific stakeholder actually cares about, and being willing to follow up rather than assuming a good idea sells itself once stated.

## Visibility of your reasoning, not just your output

A junior contributor can often get away with delivering a good result without explaining how they got there. A senior contributor is expected to make their reasoning visible — in writing, in meetings, in how they frame a recommendation — because other people need to be able to evaluate, build on, or challenge that reasoning, not just trust the final answer. If you've never had to defend a decision to a skeptical audience, that's a specific, practicable gap worth deliberately seeking out.

## Mentoring is a skill, not a side effect of experience

Many people assume they'll naturally become good at developing others once they have enough experience to share. In practice, explaining how you think through a problem — not just giving someone the answer — is a distinct skill that most people have to practice deliberately. A useful exercise: the next time you solve something for a more junior colleague, try explaining your reasoning process instead of just giving the fix, and notice how much harder that actually is.

## How to close the gap deliberately

- **Ask for ambiguous work on purpose**, rather than always gravitating toward clearly scoped tasks where you already know you'll succeed.
- **Practice writing your reasoning down**, not just your conclusion, even when nobody asked for it — a short internal document explaining why you made a call is good practice for the kind of communication senior roles require.
- **Seek out a stakeholder you have to influence, not manage**, and practice building a case rather than simply executing a request.
- **Volunteer to explain your thinking to someone more junior**, and notice where you default to giving the answer instead of the reasoning.
