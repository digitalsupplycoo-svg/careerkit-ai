How you leave a job is remembered longer than almost anything from your time there — managers move between companies, references get called years later, and industries are often smaller than they feel. Resigning professionally isn't about being falsely cheerful on the way out; it's about closing the relationship in a way that doesn't cost you anything later.

## Tell your manager before anyone else

Your direct manager should hear the news from you, directly and privately, before your team, before other departments, and before it spreads informally. Finding out secondhand — even accidentally, through a calendar invite you sent to the wrong person or a coworker who let it slip — damages the conversation you were going to have anyway and makes the whole process feel worse than it needed to.

## The conversation itself

Keep the initial conversation short, direct, and unemotional, even if the reasons you're leaving are complicated. A workable structure: state clearly that you're resigning, give your intended last day, and offer to help make the transition smooth. You don't owe a detailed explanation of every reason you're leaving, and a vague, honest version ("I've decided to take on a new opportunity") is completely acceptable even if the real story is more complicated.

## How much notice to give

Two weeks is the common default in many industries, but it's a norm, not a universal rule — some roles, especially more senior ones or those with significant handoff responsibilities, benefit from more, while some at-will arrangements (see our guide on <a href="/articles/understanding-at-will-employment-basics">understanding at-will employment</a>) technically allow less. Check your contract or offer letter for any specific notice requirement you agreed to, since some agreements specify a longer period, and honoring it protects a reference and avoids a contractual dispute.

## A simple resignation email template

> Subject: Resignation — [Your Name]
>
> Hi [Manager's name],
>
> I'm writing to let you know that I've decided to resign from my position as [Job Title], effective [last working day, typically two weeks from the send date].
>
> I'm grateful for the opportunities I've had here, and I want to make this transition as smooth as possible — please let me know how I can help hand off my current work over the next two weeks.
>
> Thank you again for everything.
>
> [Your name]

Keep the written notice this brief and neutral. It becomes a permanent record in your personnel file, so it's not the place for detailed feedback, praise, or criticism — save nuance for the verbal conversation, and save real criticism, if you have any, for an exit interview if one is offered.

## What not to say on the way out

- **Don't criticize specific people by name**, even if your frustration is justified — it rarely changes anything and can follow you if that person moves to another company later.
- **Don't announce your new employer or salary** unless asked, especially if you're leaving for a direct competitor; it can create unnecessary friction during your remaining notice period.
- **Don't disengage during your notice period.** The final two weeks are disproportionately well remembered — coasting or checking out visibly does more damage to how you're remembered than almost anything else in the entire tenure.

## If they make a counteroffer

Some employers respond to a resignation with an immediate counteroffer. It's worth pausing to genuinely ask yourself why it took a resignation to fix the issue, since the underlying reasons you were looking rarely disappear just because the pay changed — see our <a href="/tools/offer-comparator">Job Offer Comparator</a> if you're weighing a counteroffer against the new role side by side. If you accept, that's your decision to make; if you decline, doing so with the same calm, direct tone as the original resignation keeps the relationship intact either way.
