Most resumes should be chronological, and most resume advice that suggests otherwise is solving for a specific, less common situation. Understanding what each format signals helps you pick correctly instead of defaulting to whichever template you found first.

## Chronological: the default for a reason

A chronological resume lists work experience in reverse order, most recent first, with a few bullet points per role. It's the format hiring managers and ATS software are most familiar with, which means it's parsed correctly, read quickly, and doesn't require the reader to do extra work figuring out your history. If your career has a fairly continuous, logical progression in one field or a small number of related fields, chronological is almost always the right choice — there's no advantage to a more "creative" format when there's nothing to obscure.

## Functional: rarely the right call

A functional resume groups experience by skill category ("Leadership," "Project Management") instead of by job, with dates de-emphasized or moved to a brief list at the bottom. It's often recommended for people with gaps or a scattered work history, on the theory that it hides the timeline. In practice, most experienced recruiters recognize a functional resume immediately and read it as a signal that something is being minimized, which draws exactly the scrutiny it was meant to avoid. It's rarely worth using in place of a chronological resume with a well-handled gap, though it can occasionally work for a genuine career changer with strong transferable skills but limited direct experience in the target field, used briefly and combined with a clear, honest work history section.

## Hybrid: chronological with a skills-forward opening

A hybrid resume keeps the standard chronological work history but adds a short skills or "core competencies" section near the top, before the job history. This works well when your target role emphasizes specific skills that might not be obvious from job titles alone — for example, someone with a generalist "Coordinator" title who has significant, specific experience in a skill like data analysis or vendor negotiation. The hybrid format lets you surface that early without hiding the timeline the way a full functional resume does.

## Matching format to your actual situation

- **Steady, related roles in one field:** straightforward chronological.
- **A recent, well-explained gap:** chronological, with the gap formatted clearly (see the separate guide on employment gaps) rather than hidden through a different format.
- **Career change with transferable skills:** hybrid, with a skills section that connects your existing experience to the new role's requirements.
- **Early career with limited work history:** chronological, but with more emphasis on education, projects, and any relevant part-time or volunteer work, rather than switching format.
- **Long, varied career with many short roles:** chronological, but consider consolidating very old or minor roles into a single "Earlier Experience" line rather than switching formats to hide them.

## The format won't fix weak content

Whichever format you choose, the format itself doesn't compensate for vague bullet points or a scattered narrative. A well-written chronological resume with clear, results-oriented bullets will consistently outperform a cleverly formatted resume trying to work around weak content. Format is a secondary decision — get the content (what you actually did, and what changed because you did it) right first, and pick the simplest format that presents it clearly.
