Having more than one job offer at the same time is a genuinely good problem, but it comes with its own specific pressure: multiple companies wanting an answer, often on different timelines, while you're trying to make a careful decision rather than a rushed one. Handling it well means being honest with everyone involved, even while keeping some details appropriately private.

## Buy yourself time honestly, don't stall silently

If you need more time than an employer's stated deadline allows, ask directly rather than letting the deadline pass without a response: "I'm very excited about this offer, and I want to give it the consideration it deserves — would it be possible to have until [specific date] to confirm?" Most employers will grant a reasonable, specific extension; a vague, open-ended stall without a clear reason or date is more likely to frustrate them.

## You can mention a competing offer without oversharing

It's fair and common to let an employer know you're considering another offer, without disclosing the other company's name or exact number unless you choose to: "I'm weighing this against another opportunity and want to make sure I'm comparing them fairly — could you help me understand [specific thing, like total compensation or growth path] in more detail?" This is honest and can prompt a company to sharpen its offer, without requiring you to reveal confidential details from the other process.

## Using one offer to negotiate another, carefully

If you have a concrete competing offer, referencing it can be a legitimate point of leverage — but only if it's genuinely true, since a bluffed or exaggerated competing offer is easy for an experienced hiring manager to probe and can permanently damage trust if discovered. A direct, honest version: "I have another offer at $X, and I'd genuinely prefer to join your team — is there room to move closer to that number?" This works because it's specific and true, not because it sounds impressive.

## Compare offers on the same structured basis

Comparing offers purely from memory, especially under time pressure, tends to overweight whichever number you looked at most recently. ## Decide before you have to, not while on the phone

Go into any final conversation already knowing roughly where your real threshold is — the point where you'd choose one offer over another, or where a shift in a specific number or condition would change your decision. Deciding this in advance, calmly, prevents an under-pressure decision that you second-guess immediately afterward once the adrenaline of the conversation fades.

## Once you've decided, move quickly and clearly

Once you've made your choice, accept the one you want promptly and decline the others just as promptly — sitting on offers you've already mentally ruled out to keep options open longer than necessary is a real cost to the other companies and to your own reputation if word gets around, which happens more often than people expect in smaller industries.
