Most companies decline to give specific feedback after a rejection, usually for legal and liability reasons rather than indifference — an offhand comment about a candidate can become a legal exposure if it's ever characterized as discriminatory, even unintentionally, so many companies adopt a blanket policy of giving none at all. Knowing this in advance changes how to ask: not whether you'll get an answer, but how to maximize the odds of getting a genuinely useful one from a company willing to give it.

## Ask soon, and ask specifically

A generic request ("do you have any feedback?") is easy to decline with a form response. A specific, narrow question is both easier to answer and more likely to get a real one: "Was there a specific area of my background or interview that was the deciding factor?" gives the person something concrete to respond to, rather than an open-ended invitation to write an essay they don't have time for.

## A template that respects their time

> Subject: Thank you — [Role] interview
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Thank you for letting me know about the decision, and for the time you and the team spent on the process. I know feedback isn't always possible to share, but if there's anything specific about my background or interview that stood out as the deciding factor, I'd genuinely appreciate hearing it — it would help me going forward.
>
> Either way, thank you again for the opportunity, and I hope our paths cross again.
>
> [Your name]

This works because it explicitly acknowledges they might say no, asks for something specific rather than open-ended, and ends on genuine goodwill rather than visible disappointment — all of which make it easier for someone to actually respond.

## What to do with a generic non-answer

Most responses will be some version of "we had many strong candidates and made a difficult decision" — genuinely uninformative, and not worth pushing back on. Don't ask a second time or push for more detail; a repeated request after a generic decline reads as pressure, not genuine curiosity, and can affect whether they'd consider you for a future role.

## What to do with real, specific feedback

If you do get something specific and useful, resist the urge to argue or explain it away, even if you disagree — the goal of the conversation was information, not a chance to relitigate the decision. Write it down, and if there's a real pattern across specific feedback from more than one rejection, treat it as a genuine signal worth addressing before your next several applications, not a one-off comment to dismiss.

## When silence itself is the feedback

If a company doesn't respond to a feedback request at all, that's not a sign anything went specifically wrong beyond a normal decline — many hiring teams are simply moving fast onto other things and don't reply to any post-decision emails, whether or not the request included a feedback question. Treat non-response the same as a generic "not moving forward" and continue your search without reading additional meaning into the silence.

## Where this fits into a broader search

Feedback requests are a small, low-cost habit worth doing consistently, not a strategy to lean on heavily — most attempts get nothing usable, but the occasional specific, useful response is worth the minimal effort it takes to ask.
